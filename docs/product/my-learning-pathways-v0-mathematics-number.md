# My Learning Pathways V0 - Mathematics F-10 / K-10

This document defines MyLearna's first guided pathway specification for mathematics. It is a product and education specification only. It does not add UI, routes, schema, storage, or runtime behavior.

This pathway framework is MyLearna-owned language, informed by and crosswalked against:

- Australian Curriculum: Mathematics F-10
- Australian Curriculum Numeracy learning progression
- Florida B.E.S.T. Standards for Mathematics
- UK National Curriculum Mathematics KS1, KS2, KS3 and KS4
- UK Mathematics Appendix 1 formal written methods

It is designed to translate curriculum expectations into parent-followable next steps without claiming official endorsement, approval, or guaranteed compliance.

## 1. Product purpose

Homeschool parents often know that mathematics matters, but they do not always know:

- what should come next
- why that next step matters
- what solid practice looks like
- how to notice progress early
- how to capture evidence without turning family learning into a school administration task

Raw standards documents are usually written for teachers, systems, inspectors or curriculum teams. They are useful reference points, but they are not always easy for a non-teacher parent to turn into a calm weekly plan.

My Learning Pathways is intended to solve that translation problem. It turns complex standards and progressions into a guided sequence that a parent can follow step by step. The pathway should help a family understand:

- the current skill focus
- the likely prerequisite beneath it
- the most likely next step after it
- how to practise the skill
- what evidence could show it
- what later assessment checks might look like

In the wider product, pathways are the layer that connects "what matters educationally" to "what should happen next in real family planning."

## 2. Relationship to existing MyLearna layers

Each MyLearna layer answers a different question:

- My Curriculum answers: "What learning areas and expectations have evidence building already?"
- My Assessments answers: "How confident does this learner currently look in a specific skill or stage?"
- My Learning Pathways answers: "What is the most likely next skill step, and what usually comes after that?"
- My Programs answers: "How do we turn pathway steps into a structured plan?"
- My Calendar and My Day answer: "When is this learning happening?"
- My Capture answers: "What evidence did we see during the learning?"
- My Reports and My Outputs answer: "How do we communicate progress, coverage and evidence clearly?"

Productly, pathways should become the educational spine that sits between coverage and planning:

- coverage says what has been touched
- assessments say how secure a skill looks
- pathways suggest what to do next
- programs and calendar turn that suggestion into a real plan
- capture, reports and outputs preserve the record

## 3. Full Mathematics F-10 / K-10 domain map

MyLearna should use a broad, parent-readable mathematics map that can later be crosswalked to the selected curriculum framework in My Settings.

### Number and place value

- Parent-friendly description: understanding quantity, counting, numeral meaning, magnitude, ordering and how our number system is built.
- Why it matters: this is the base for later addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, fractions, decimals, ratio, algebra and financial maths.
- Broad progression: begins with counting and matching number names to quantities, grows into place value, regrouping, larger numbers, decimals, negative numbers, index notation and efficient number choice in complex problems.
- MyLearna pathway status: first detailed strand.

### Operations and calculation

- Parent-friendly description: using addition, subtraction, multiplication and division with growing accuracy, flexibility and efficiency.
- Why it matters: learners need both conceptual understanding and usable methods, including mental strategies, informal written methods and later formal algorithms.
- Broad progression: starts with joining and separating collections, moves to number facts and place-value strategies, then develops reliable and fluent written methods, multi-step calculation and strategy choice.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Fractions, decimals, percentages

- Parent-friendly description: understanding parts of a whole, equivalent representations and proportional quantities.
- Why it matters: this domain unlocks measurement, money, probability, ratio, algebra and later secondary number work.
- Broad progression: starts with sharing and equal parts, then fraction naming, equivalence, decimal place value, percent links, operations with rational numbers and exact calculation.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Ratio and proportional reasoning

- Parent-friendly description: comparing quantities multiplicatively and reasoning about scale, rate, proportion and growth.
- Why it matters: ratio is central to finance, maps, scale, recipes, speed, graphs, algebra and scientific thinking.
- Broad progression: begins informally through equal groups and sharing, develops through multiplicative thinking and percent links, then expands into rate, scaling, direct proportion, percentage change and applied modelling.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Algebra, patterns and functions

- Parent-friendly description: noticing structure, describing rules, using symbols and reasoning about change and relationships.
- Why it matters: algebra is the bridge from arithmetic to generalisation, equations, graphing and formal mathematical modelling.
- Broad progression: begins with repeating patterns and missing-number relationships, grows into unknowns and equivalence, then into expressions, equations, functions, graphs and symbolic manipulation.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Measurement

- Parent-friendly description: comparing and quantifying attributes such as length, mass, capacity, time, area, perimeter, volume and angle.
- Why it matters: measurement connects mathematics to the physical world and makes number useful in everyday decisions.
- Broad progression: starts with direct comparison and informal units, moves to standard units and elapsed time, then to area, volume, scale, formula use and practical accuracy.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Geometry and spatial reasoning

- Parent-friendly description: thinking about shape, position, movement, angle, symmetry, transformation and spatial structure.
- Why it matters: spatial reasoning supports design, diagrams, maps, construction, science, technology and later graphing and trigonometric ideas.
- Broad progression: begins with naming and sorting familiar shapes, then describing properties and position, and later extends into transformations, similarity, construction, coordinates and geometric proof habits.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Statistics and data

- Parent-friendly description: collecting, organising, representing, interpreting and critiquing data.
- Why it matters: children need to make sense of information, claims, graphs and patterns in a data-rich world.
- Broad progression: starts with sorting and simple displays, grows into tables and graphs, then into averages, variation, comparison, trend, interpretation and data-informed argument.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Probability and chance

- Parent-friendly description: understanding randomness, likelihood, prediction and uncertainty.
- Why it matters: probability supports risk thinking, decision-making, statistics and critical reading of claims.
- Broad progression: begins with informal chance language, then simple experiments and likelihood comparison, and later extends into sample spaces, theoretical probability and applied uncertainty.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Financial and real-world mathematics

- Parent-friendly description: applying number, measure, ratio and reasoning to money, budgeting, pricing, percentage change and practical decisions.
- Why it matters: families often want mathematics to feel relevant, useful and connected to everyday life.
- Broad progression: starts with coin recognition and simple money totals, grows into budgeting, percent links and comparison shopping, then into tax-style reasoning, interest-style growth, scaling and practical modelling.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

### Mathematical reasoning, modelling and explanation

- Parent-friendly description: explaining why an answer makes sense, choosing methods, testing ideas and using mathematics to represent real situations.
- Why it matters: this is the difference between following a procedure and actually understanding mathematics.
- Broad progression: begins with describing what was counted or compared, develops into explaining methods and checking reasonableness, then extends into justification, modelling assumptions, accuracy limits and argument.
- MyLearna pathway status: future detailed strand.

## 4. Why Number is the first detailed pathway

Number should be the first detailed My Learning Pathways strand because:

- Number is foundational. A weak number spine weakens later operations, fractions, algebra, measurement and financial mathematics.
- Number is highly sequential. Parents can usually understand "this comes before that" much more clearly in number than in some other domains.
- Number is broadly consistent across frameworks. Australian, UK and Florida references all place strong emphasis on counting, place value, operations, fraction sense, proportional thinking and later secondary number fluency.
- Number is highly assessable in family settings. Parents can observe it through talk, manipulatives, written work, practical tasks and everyday routines.
- Number connects naturally to evidence capture. It is easy to document with photos, short notes, worked examples, oral explanations and practical tasks.
- Number gives MyLearna a strong pathway prototype. If the product can guide parents clearly through number progression, the same pattern can later be extended to measurement, algebra, geometry and data.

This V0 spec therefore treats "Number" as a broad number spine rather than a narrow place-value-only strand. It begins with quantity and counting, then grows through operations, rational number, proportional thinking and later secondary number application.

## 5. Definition of a pathway step

Each MyLearna pathway step should be a parent-usable unit of progression, not just a curriculum topic label.

Every step should include:

- Step title: the parent-facing name of the learning step.
- What it means: a short explanation of the concept in everyday language.
- What the learner should be able to do: observable success indicators.
- Prerequisite: the most likely earlier understanding needed before this step becomes secure.
- Next likely step: the most probable immediate progression after this step.
- Practice ideas: low-friction activities a parent can use at home.
- Evidence examples: realistic capture items that could be saved in MyLearna.
- Future assessment check idea: a short check prompt or task that could later power My Assessments.
- Crosswalk notes: how the step later maps to major frameworks without exposing the parent to a standards database.

Design rules for pathway steps:

- a step should be small enough to act on
- a step should be observable in real family learning
- a step should describe both understanding and use
- a step should support different teaching styles and materials
- a step should connect forward to planning, assessment and evidence
- a step should remain framework-neutral at the parent surface, even when a crosswalk exists underneath

## 6. MyLearna Number Pathway - parent-followable sequence

### Pathway design notes

- Stage labels are broad MyLearna bands, not official year-level replacements.
- The product should later map these steps to the selected curriculum expectations in My Settings.
- Steps move from concrete quantity to abstract number, from additive to multiplicative thinking, and from familiar problems to more complex modelling and reasoning.
- Operations, fractions and proportional reasoning appear inside this number spine because they are strongly dependent on number development and are highly consistent across the reference frameworks.

### Foundation / Kindergarten

#### Step 1. Recognise small quantities without counting

- What it means: the learner can see very small groups and know "how many" straight away.
- The learner should be able to do: instantly recognise common groups up to about 4 or 5 and describe what they notice.
- Prerequisite: none beyond everyday exposure to collections and quantity language.
- Next likely step: Step 2 - Match spoken number names to quantities.
- Practice ideas: dot cards, dice flashes, finger patterns, quick-look counters.
- Evidence examples: photo of a dot-card response, short capture note, audio clip explaining "I saw three and one more."
- Future assessment check idea: briefly show 4 objects and ask how many without counting aloud.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with early quantity recognition, subitising and counting-readiness expectations across Australian Foundation, UK early number / KS1 entry work, and Florida K NSO.

#### Step 2. Match spoken number names to quantities

- What it means: the learner connects number words such as "three" or "seven" to actual amounts.
- The learner should be able to do: select or build a collection to match a spoken number in a familiar range.
- Prerequisite: Step 1 or similar quantity awareness.
- Next likely step: Step 3 - Identify numerals 0-10.
- Practice ideas: "show me" games, snack counts, toy collections, number song pauses.
- Evidence examples: video of the learner making 6 with counters, parent note from a play activity.
- Future assessment check idea: say a number from 0-10 and ask the learner to build that many objects.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to early number-name, counting and quantity connections in Australian Foundation, UK KS1 opening expectations and Florida K NSO.

#### Step 3. Identify numerals 0-10

- What it means: the learner recognises written numerals and links them to quantity.
- The learner should be able to do: name numerals 0-10, match them to sets and notice zero as "none."
- Prerequisite: Step 2.
- Next likely step: Step 4 - Count objects accurately to 10.
- Practice ideas: numeral hunts, number cards, matching games, bath or chalk numbers.
- Evidence examples: photo of numeral matching, short worksheet sample, verbal explanation about zero.
- Future assessment check idea: present mixed numeral cards 0-10 and ask the learner to point to named numerals and match sets.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with numeral recognition and quantity matching in Australian Foundation, UK KS1 early number and Florida K counting/place-value expectations.

#### Step 4. Count objects accurately to 10

- What it means: the learner counts one object once, in order, and finishes with the total.
- The learner should be able to do: count collections to 10 with one-to-one matching and say how many altogether.
- Prerequisite: Step 3.
- Next likely step: Step 5 - Count objects accurately to 20.
- Practice ideas: move-and-count games, collecting leaves, lining up toys, counting spoons or blocks.
- Evidence examples: capture note from a counting activity, short video, annotated photo of counted objects.
- Future assessment check idea: give a mixed arrangement of up to 10 objects and ask the learner to count and state the total.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to counting processes and cardinality across Australian Foundation, UK Year 1 early number and Florida K NSO.

#### Step 5. Count objects accurately to 20

- What it means: the learner extends one-to-one counting beyond 10 while keeping track of the total.
- The learner should be able to do: count to 20 in order, count collections to 20 and recover after a small pause or prompt.
- Prerequisite: Step 4.
- Next likely step: Step 6 - Compare groups as more, fewer or same.
- Practice ideas: ten-frame extensions, stair counts, hidden-count collections, outdoor count hunts.
- Evidence examples: photo of teen-number counting, parent note about counting a larger group, whiteboard recording.
- Future assessment check idea: present 14 to 20 objects and ask the learner to count accurately and explain the final answer.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Foundation end-of-year number sequence expectations, UK KS1 early counting development and Florida K count-to-100 readiness.

#### Step 6. Compare groups as more, fewer or same

- What it means: the learner notices relative quantity and can compare two collections.
- The learner should be able to do: tell which group has more, fewer or the same, using matching, counting or visual comparison.
- Prerequisite: Step 5.
- Next likely step: Step 7 - Order numbers in a short sequence.
- Practice ideas: compare snack bowls, line up tower heights, matching socks or counters, simple "who has more?" games.
- Evidence examples: photo comparison with learner explanation, capture note on quantity language used.
- Future assessment check idea: show two collections and ask which has more, fewer or the same and how the learner knows.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to comparison and magnitude expectations in Australian Foundation, UK KS1 number comparison and Florida K NSO.

#### Step 7. Order numbers in a short sequence

- What it means: the learner can place small numbers in order and notice which comes before or after.
- The learner should be able to do: arrange numerals or quantities in a short ascending or descending sequence in a familiar range.
- Prerequisite: Step 6.
- Next likely step: Step 8 - Partition and combine small collections up to 10.
- Practice ideas: numeral strips, peg lines, stair steps, "what number is missing?" games.
- Evidence examples: photo of ordered numeral cards, parent note from a puzzle activity, short worksheet.
- Future assessment check idea: give mixed cards 0-10 and ask the learner to place them in order and identify a missing number.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with ordering and sequencing in Australian Foundation, UK KS1 number order and Florida K / Grade 1 early number progression.

#### Step 8. Partition and combine small collections up to 10

- What it means: the learner understands that one total can be made in different ways.
- The learner should be able to do: show several ways to make a number to 10 using objects, fingers, drawings or spoken explanations.
- Prerequisite: Step 7.
- Next likely step: Step 9 - Represent simple addition/subtraction stories with objects.
- Practice ideas: part-whole mats, five- and ten-frame games, "make 7 another way," finger combinations.
- Evidence examples: photo of split collections, learner dictation, whiteboard part-whole drawing.
- Future assessment check idea: ask for two different ways to make 8 and how the learner knows the whole stays the same.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian Foundation partitioning work, UK KS1 composition of number and Florida K / Grade 1 exploration of early operations.

#### Step 9. Represent simple addition/subtraction stories with objects

- What it means: the learner connects everyday joining and taking-away situations to number action.
- The learner should be able to do: act out simple stories, show them with objects or drawings and describe what happened.
- Prerequisite: Step 8.
- Next likely step: Step 10 - Share small collections equally.
- Practice ideas: story mats, toys in and out of boxes, snack stories, simple number books.
- Evidence examples: short video of story acting, parent note with learner language, drawing showing the action.
- Future assessment check idea: tell a short story such as "3 birds and 2 more came" and ask the learner to model it.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Foundation and KS1 early additive thinking, and Florida K / Grade 1 exploration benchmarks using objects, drawings and equations.

#### Step 10. Share small collections equally

- What it means: the learner begins to understand fair sharing and equal groups.
- The learner should be able to do: share small sets between 2 or more groups and recognise when the shares are equal or not equal.
- Prerequisite: Step 9.
- Next likely step: Step 11 - Count forwards and backwards within 100/120.
- Practice ideas: teddy picnics, snack sharing, plate-and-counter games, simple equal-group stories.
- Evidence examples: photo of equal shares, parent note from a snack activity, short explanation of what made the sharing fair.
- Future assessment check idea: give 8 counters and ask the learner to share them fairly between 2 groups.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to early multiplicative thinking and equal sharing in Australian Foundation / Year 1, UK KS1 and Florida K / Grade 1 operations progression.

### Lower Primary

#### Step 11. Count forwards and backwards within 100/120

- What it means: the learner extends the number sequence and begins counting on and back from given points.
- The learner should be able to do: count forwards and backwards across decade boundaries and start from a number other than 1.
- Prerequisite: Step 10.
- Next likely step: Step 12 - Read, write and order numbers to 100/120.
- Practice ideas: hundreds chart games, stair counting, beanbag countdowns, number-line jumps.
- Evidence examples: video of counting across 39 to 40 or 100 to 99, capture note from a chart game.
- Future assessment check idea: start at 47 and ask the learner to count on ten numbers, then back five numbers.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Years 1-2 number sequence expectations, UK Year 1 and Year 2 counting progression, and Florida K / Grade 1 recitation and counting-on benchmarks.

#### Step 12. Read, write and order numbers to 100/120

- What it means: the learner uses numerals meaningfully in a larger range and compares numbers by value, not just by size of digits seen separately.
- The learner should be able to do: read, write, compare and order numbers in a two-digit range and explain which is larger.
- Prerequisite: Step 11.
- Next likely step: Step 13 - Skip count by 2s, 5s and 10s.
- Practice ideas: number-card races, missing-number charts, ordering cards, house-number walks.
- Evidence examples: ordered card strip, annotated number line, audio explanation of which number is greater.
- Future assessment check idea: give three two-digit numbers and ask the learner to order them smallest to largest and explain why.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian Year 1 place-value development, UK Year 1-2 number and place value, and Florida Grade 1-2 NSO expectations.

#### Step 13. Skip count by 2s, 5s and 10s

- What it means: the learner begins to treat number as patterned groups instead of single steps only.
- The learner should be able to do: count in 2s, 5s and 10s forward, connect skip counting to grouping and notice repeated patterns.
- Prerequisite: Step 12.
- Next likely step: Step 14 - Understand ten ones make one ten.
- Practice ideas: clapping patterns, bundled sticks, counting coins, array walks, bead strings.
- Evidence examples: skip-count recording, video of counting groups, parent note about grouping objects.
- Future assessment check idea: show groups of 5 and ask the learner to count the total by fives without counting one by one.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Years 1-2 grouping and counting in multiples, UK KS1 counting in multiples and Florida Grade 1-2 NSO.

#### Step 14. Understand ten ones make one ten

- What it means: the learner grasps the base-ten structure beneath two-digit numbers.
- The learner should be able to do: bundle ten ones into one ten and explain that the ten still represents ten objects.
- Prerequisite: Step 13.
- Next likely step: Step 15 - Partition two-digit numbers into tens and ones.
- Practice ideas: straws and bundles, linking cubes, ten frames, base-ten blocks, trading games.
- Evidence examples: photo of bundled materials, capture note using tens language, learner explanation.
- Future assessment check idea: ask the learner to build 23 and explain how many tens and ones it has.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian Year 1-2 place-value structure, UK Year 1 and Year 2 place value, and Florida Grade 1-2 place-value benchmarks.

#### Step 15. Partition two-digit numbers into tens and ones

- What it means: the learner breaks a number into place-value parts.
- The learner should be able to do: represent numbers like 46 as 4 tens and 6 ones using materials, drawings or equations.
- Prerequisite: Step 14.
- Next likely step: Step 16 - Rename two-digit numbers in different ways.
- Practice ideas: build-and-break tasks, place-value mats, dice number builds, mini whiteboard partitioning.
- Evidence examples: base-ten photo, written partitioning, short video using place-value language.
- Future assessment check idea: give a number such as 58 and ask for tens and ones in at least one representation.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Years 1-2 partitioning, UK Year 2 place value and Florida Grade 2 NSO decomposition work.

#### Step 16. Rename two-digit numbers in different ways

- What it means: the learner sees that numbers can be decomposed flexibly, not only in standard tens-and-ones form.
- The learner should be able to do: rename 34 as 3 tens and 4 ones, or 2 tens and 14 ones, with support.
- Prerequisite: Step 15.
- Next likely step: Step 17 - Add and subtract within 20 using known facts.
- Practice ideas: trading games, regrouping with bundles, "another way to make this number" prompts.
- Evidence examples: written decompositions, photo of regrouped materials, parent note about flexible partitioning.
- Future assessment check idea: ask for two different ways to represent 42 and explain why both are still 42.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian Years 2-3 regrouping ideas, UK Year 2 place-value flexibility and Florida Grade 2-3 NSO composition and decomposition.

#### Step 17. Add and subtract within 20 using known facts

- What it means: the learner begins to use efficient fact-based thinking rather than counting every object one by one.
- The learner should be able to do: use number bonds, doubles, near doubles or make-ten strategies within 20.
- Prerequisite: Step 16.
- Next likely step: Step 18 - Add and subtract one- and two-digit numbers with support.
- Practice ideas: ten-frame fact games, fact families, card sums, "make ten then add on."
- Evidence examples: worked example, oral explanation of strategy, short capture note about a fact used.
- Future assessment check idea: ask how to solve 8 + 6 or 15 - 7 and listen for strategy rather than only the answer.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian additive strategies, UK KS1 calculation fluency and Florida recall / reliability stages for early addition and subtraction.

#### Step 18. Add and subtract one- and two-digit numbers with support

- What it means: the learner applies place-value and fact knowledge to larger calculations.
- The learner should be able to do: solve simple one- and two-digit addition and subtraction problems using drawings, number lines, materials or supported written methods.
- Prerequisite: Step 17.
- Next likely step: Step 19 - Understand simple equal groups and arrays.
- Practice ideas: number lines, open number sentences, base-ten block jumps, story problems with regrouping support.
- Evidence examples: annotated number line, base-ten photo, parent note on strategy choice.
- Future assessment check idea: give a calculation such as 34 + 12 or 51 - 19 and ask the learner to show a workable method.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian Years 1-2 modelling and calculation, UK Year 2 addition/subtraction methods and Florida procedural reliability benchmarks.

#### Step 19. Understand simple equal groups and arrays

- What it means: the learner begins to see multiplication and division as structured grouping.
- The learner should be able to do: make equal groups, read simple arrays and connect repeated addition to grouping.
- Prerequisite: Step 18.
- Next likely step: Step 20 - Begin halves, quarters and simple sharing.
- Practice ideas: egg-carton arrays, muffin trays, equal-group stories, counters in rows and columns.
- Evidence examples: array photo, learner explanation, quick sketch of groups and totals.
- Future assessment check idea: show 3 rows of 4 and ask how many altogether and how the learner knows.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Years 1-2 equal grouping, UK KS1 multiplication foundations and Florida Grade 2-3 multiplication preparation.

#### Step 20. Begin halves, quarters and simple sharing

- What it means: the learner starts linking fair sharing to fraction language and simple part-whole thinking.
- The learner should be able to do: share simple sets equally, describe halves and quarters in familiar shapes or collections and connect sharing to equal parts.
- Prerequisite: Step 19.
- Next likely step: Step 21 - Read, write, order and compare numbers to 1000 and beyond.
- Practice ideas: food sharing, folding paper, partitioning shapes, cut-and-share stories.
- Evidence examples: photo of shared objects, shape partition drawing, capture note with fraction language.
- Future assessment check idea: ask the learner to show half of 12 or quarter a simple shape and explain why the parts are equal.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian early fraction development, UK Year 1-2 halves and quarters, and Florida fraction-readiness work in lower primary.

### Middle Primary

#### Step 21. Read, write, order and compare numbers to 1000 and beyond

- What it means: the learner extends number sense into the hundreds and larger numbers with stable ordering.
- The learner should be able to do: read, write, compare and order three-digit numbers and some larger numbers in context.
- Prerequisite: Step 20.
- Next likely step: Step 22 - Understand hundreds, tens and ones.
- Practice ideas: place-value charts, number-line placements, ordering cards, "which is larger and why?" games.
- Evidence examples: written ordering task, audio explanation, capture note from a place-value game.
- Future assessment check idea: provide several three-digit numbers and ask the learner to order them and justify the largest.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Years 2-4 place value, UK lower KS2 place-value extension and Florida Grade 3 NSO.

#### Step 22. Understand hundreds, tens and ones

- What it means: the learner extends base-ten understanding into three-digit numbers.
- The learner should be able to do: explain the value of digits in the hundreds, tens and ones places and build numbers with grouped materials.
- Prerequisite: Step 21.
- Next likely step: Step 23 - Partition and regroup two- and three-digit numbers.
- Practice ideas: base-ten blocks, bundling straws, digital place-value tools, build-and-say tasks.
- Evidence examples: place-value build photo, learner oral explanation, annotated model.
- Future assessment check idea: show 362 and ask what each digit represents and how to build it.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian number-and-place-value development, UK lower KS2 place value and Florida Grade 3 NSO decomposition.

#### Step 23. Partition and regroup two- and three-digit numbers

- What it means: the learner flexibly breaks apart and renames larger numbers to support calculation.
- The learner should be able to do: decompose numbers in more than one way, including regrouping one hundred as ten tens or one ten as ten ones.
- Prerequisite: Step 22.
- Next likely step: Step 24 - Use zero as a placeholder.
- Practice ideas: regrouping exchanges, place-value trading, "show 245 another way," draw-and-rename tasks.
- Evidence examples: regrouped block representation, parent note on exchanged tens and ones, written decomposition.
- Future assessment check idea: ask for two different decompositions of 305 or 472 and why both are valid.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian Years 3-4 regrouping, UK lower KS2 place-value reasoning and Florida Grade 3-4 NSO.

#### Step 24. Use zero as a placeholder

- What it means: the learner understands that zero can hold an empty place in the number system.
- The learner should be able to do: interpret numbers such as 204 or 3.05 correctly and explain the role of zero.
- Prerequisite: Step 23.
- Next likely step: Step 25 - Add and subtract two- and three-digit numbers using place value.
- Practice ideas: compare 24 and 204, build missing-place numbers, place-value chart games, read-and-write tasks.
- Evidence examples: audio explanation of 406, written comparison, capture note from a place-value activity.
- Future assessment check idea: ask what the zero means in 507 and what changes if it is removed.
- Crosswalk notes: maps to Australian and UK place-value understanding in primary, and Florida place-value benchmarks that stress decomposition and representation.

#### Step 25. Add and subtract two- and three-digit numbers using place value

- What it means: the learner uses place-value strategies, not only counting, to solve larger calculations.
- The learner should be able to do: add and subtract two- and three-digit numbers using partitioning, compensation, open number lines or supported written methods.
- Prerequisite: Step 24.
- Next likely step: Step 26 - Recall and apply multiplication facts.
- Practice ideas: expanded form calculation, jump strategies, regrouping with blocks, practical story problems.
- Evidence examples: written method sample, learner explanation, parent note on strategy choice.
- Future assessment check idea: ask the learner to solve 236 + 147 or 402 - 185 and explain the method used.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian additive strategies and modelling, UK lower KS2 calculation methods, and Florida procedural reliability into fluency.

#### Step 26. Recall and apply multiplication facts

- What it means: the learner can quickly use multiplication and related division facts in practical work.
- The learner should be able to do: recall key facts, use commutativity, connect related facts and apply them in short problems.
- Prerequisite: Step 25.
- Next likely step: Step 27 - Multiply and divide using arrays, grouping and known facts.
- Practice ideas: fact families, array cards, skip-count rhythm games, multiplication grids.
- Evidence examples: fact recall sample, parent note on efficient recall, explanation of a related fact.
- Future assessment check idea: ask for quick solutions to a small set of times-table and related division prompts.
- Crosswalk notes: aligns with Australian multiplicative strategies, UK KS2 multiplication-table fluency and Florida automaticity with multiplication facts.

#### Step 27. Multiply and divide using arrays, grouping and known facts

- What it means: the learner uses structure to solve multiplication and division rather than treating them as unrelated facts.
- The learner should be able to do: solve equal-group, array and sharing problems and connect multiplication with division.
- Prerequisite: Step 26.
- Next likely step: Step 28 - Estimate and check reasonableness.
- Practice ideas: array building, grouped collections, inverse-story problems, unknown-group tasks.
- Evidence examples: array drawing, grouped object photo, capture note explaining inverse relationships.
- Future assessment check idea: present an equal-groups problem and ask for both a multiplication and related division interpretation.

## 7. Crosswalk principles

MyLearna should later map pathway steps to frameworks using the following principles.

### 7.1 MyLearna steps stay parent-facing and framework-neutral

Parents should see calm, actionable step language such as:

- "Partition two-digit numbers into tens and ones"
- "Interpret remainders in context"
- "Use percentage change in practical problems"

They should not need to navigate curriculum codes or benchmark databases to understand what to do next.

### 7.2 Crosswalks are one-to-many, not one-to-one

One MyLearna pathway step may connect to:

- an Australian content description
- part of an achievement standard
- one or more Numeracy progression indicators
- a UK year-level or key-stage expectation
- a Florida benchmark family

The crosswalk should therefore be concept-based, not code-first.

### 7.3 Sequence comes before code matching

MyLearna should protect the learning sequence even when frameworks organise content differently. For example:

- Australian Curriculum separates Number and Algebra but emphasises their connections.
- UK programmes of study foreground number, fluency and formal methods within year bands and key stages.
- Florida B.E.S.T. uses strand and benchmark structures with explicit development from exploration to reliability to fluency and automaticity.

The internal crosswalk should not break a parent-friendly learning sequence just to mirror a standards index.

### 7.4 Australian Curriculum mapping principle

Each pathway step can later be mapped to:

- Australian Mathematics content descriptions
- year-level achievement standard language
- broader strand placement across Number, Algebra, Measurement, Space, Statistics and Probability

The Number pathway will most often anchor in the Number strand, while later steps also connect outward to Algebra, Measurement and modelling.

### 7.5 Australian Numeracy progression mapping principle

Each pathway step can later be tagged against Numeracy progression sub-elements such as:

- Number and place value
- Counting processes
- Additive strategies
- Multiplicative strategies
- Interpreting fractions
- Proportional thinking
- Number patterns and algebraic thinking
- Understanding money

This progression can help MyLearna estimate "what usually comes next" even when a learner is uneven across age bands.

### 7.6 UK National Curriculum mapping principle

Each pathway step can later map to:

- KS1 year-by-year number and calculation expectations
- lower KS2 and upper KS2 place value, fractions and operations expectations
- KS3 number, proportional reasoning and algebra-readiness expectations
- KS4 number fluency, standard form, exact calculation, percentage change and limits of accuracy

Appendix 1 should also inform the later calculation-method guidance for operations steps, especially when MyLearna eventually specifies formal written method expectations.

### 7.7 Florida B.E.S.T. mapping principle

Each pathway step can later map to:

- Number Sense and Operations benchmarks
- connected Algebraic Reasoning benchmarks where number work supports equation or pattern reasoning
- Mathematical Thinking and Reasoning expectations around explanation, modelling and problem solving

The Florida design language of exploration, procedural reliability, procedural fluency and automaticity is especially useful when MyLearna later writes progression-sensitive practice and assessment checks.

### 7.8 Later selected US state and DoDEA-informed mapping principle

MyLearna should not hardcode a state-by-state standards engine in V0. Instead it should:

- keep the pathway steps as the stable parent-facing layer
- map them later to selected state or DoDEA-informed expectations through concept families
- use stage bands and dependencies rather than attempting a giant code mirror

This keeps the product maintainable while still supporting credible framework-aware reporting later.

### 7.9 Product implementation principle for later phases

When this specification becomes product logic later:

- My Learning Pathways should suggest next steps
- My Assessments should record confidence against those steps
- My Programs should turn chosen steps into plan items
- My Calendar and My Day should schedule those plan items
- My Capture should gather evidence against real pathway steps
- My Reports and My Outputs should explain progress using pathway language plus framework crosswalks where needed

The parent experience should stay simple even when the backend crosswalk becomes sophisticated.
